DevMatch

A platform that matches developers to teams by what the team is missing, not by who ranks highest.

On most hiring platforms, teams see the top-ranked developers first, cherry-pick them, and junior developers get left out. DevMatch recommends developers by the gap in a team: role first, then experience, then skill score. Teams can send offers to developers, developers can apply to teams, and both sides can message in real time.

We built it to a specification written by another student team acting as our client, and reviewed progress with them in three meetings. I owned the frontend architecture: the dashboard, public and private profiles, the offer workflow, and the state management behind two-way applications.

What changed along the way

Midway through, the client asked for private profiles to stay hidden from browsing but remain reachable by the matching engine, so we separated who can see a profile from who can be recommended. Testing also caught a real bug: the AI analysis returned different skill scores for the same profile on repeated runs. Casual use would never have shown it, and fixing it made the core feature reliable.

Meros

A pendant light about heritage: fragments that remember the whole.

Meros means heritage in Uzbek. The Persian Empire no longer exists, but its ideas didn’t disappear. They live on in other cultures, including the architecture of Uzbekistan, where I come from.

The lamp is a star built from rhombic panels. Each panel carries a tiling of smaller stars, so every fragment still holds the pattern of the whole. The geometry comes from Persian tilework, which used endless patterns to suggest infinity; Art Deco gave it discipline, with strict symmetry and a radiating silhouette.

I modeled the geometry and a clip-together joint system in Rhino, so the panels assemble into a star and come apart again. Every panel was 3D printed and assembled by hand.

I used AI to generate an early concept sketch and the two scene visualizations above. The pattern, geometry, joints, modeling, and fabrication are my own.

Adjustable table lamp

A spring-balanced desk lamp that shows its mechanism instead of hiding it.

The lamp follows George Carwardine’s 1932 Anglepoise, where springs balance the arm so it holds any position. Rather than the clean, hidden-mechanism look of most modern lamps, I took a postmodern approach: the springs, bolts, and linkages are left exposed and become the lamp’s visual language, wrapped around a slim LED head.

I modeled the full assembly in Fusion 360, from the parallel arm linkages and spring mounts to the head housing, then animated it to test how the arm moves. Standard hardware such as screws, nuts, springs, and the switch came from component libraries. The project won second place in the Autodesk Design Competition.
